07 Where RAG stops working Agenda

Where RAG stops working

Some problems have known fixes. Some are built into the design.

    Two kinds of RAG problems

    Some come from how the pipeline is built, and better engineering fixes them. Others come from the design itself: the model only sees the few chunks that look most like the question.

    Fixable with better retrieval

    1Chunks that don't stand aloneCut mid-rule, or missing what they're about.
    2Exact names and codes missedVectors capture meaning, not "HR-17".
    3Similar isn't relevantOld versions and near-misses rank first.
    4A stale indexThe file changed; the chunks didn't.
    5No way to tell it worksAnswers read well while the right chunk never arrived.

    Built into the design

    6Connected factsThe answer is a chain across documents.
    7The whole collection"Main themes?" can't come from five chunks.
    8Logic and numbersSimilarity can't apply "more than 10".
    Each fixable problem is shown first, then its fix. The last three lead to KAG and GraphRAG.

    1. Chunks that don't stand alone

    Cutting every 60 words is simple, but the cut falls wherever it falls.

    Problem: fixed-size cuts

    Chunk 12…25 days of paid leave. 4. Carry-over. Up to 5 unused days may be carried into the next year. They must be used by
    cut
    Chunk 1331 March and need the line manager's approval. 5. Sick leave. Report your absence on the first day…

    Chunk 12 mixes two topics. Chunk 13 says "by 31 March" without saying what for, so a question about carry-over may never find it.

    Fix: cut by structure, then add context

    Context, written by an LLMFrom ACME's 2025 Leave Policy, section 4, on carrying over unused leave.
    4. Carry-over. Up to 5 unused days may be carried into the next year. They must be used by 31 March and need the line manager's approval.
    sourceLeave policysection4version2025audienceall staff

    One section, one chunk. A short context line says where it comes from, and labels allow filters and citations.

    Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval (Sept 2024): adding 50 to 100 tokens of context to each chunk cut failed retrievals by 35%; by 49% when combined with keyword search, and 67% with reranking too.

    2. Exact names and codes are missed

    "Who signs form HR-17?" To a vector, "HR-17" means little: every chunk about forms looks alike.

    Problem: vector search alone

    1Forms General rules for forms and approvals0.82
    2Leave §2 How to submit a leave request form0.80
    3Expenses §3 Who approves expense form EX-20.79
    …5 more
    9Leave §6 Form HR-17 is signed by the HR director0.71

    The one chunk that answers ranks 9th, outside the top 3.

    Fix: hybrid search

    Keyword search

    1Leave §6 HR-17
    2Forms form

    Vector search

    1Forms
    2Leave §2
    ↓ merged into one ranking
    1Leave §6 Form HR-17 is signed by the HR director✓
    2Forms General rules for forms and approvals
    3Leave §2 How to submit a leave request form
    Keyword search (usually BM25) catches names, codes and numbers; vectors catch meaning. Most search engines and vector databases can run both and merge the results.

    3. Similar isn't relevant

    "How many unused days can I carry over?" The top result is confidently wrong.

    Problem: the top 3 by similarity

    1Leave policy 2023 Up to 3 unused days…0.90
    2Leave policy 2025 Up to 5 unused days…0.89
    3Expenses §7 Unused travel budget does not carry over0.74

    The replaced 2023 policy is still in the index and wins by a hair. A travel rule sneaks in on shared words.

    Fix: filter by labels, then rerank

    1. Filter: version = current
    Leave policy 2023 Up to 3 unused days…
    2. Rerank: a second model reads the question with each chunk
    1Leave policy 2025 Up to 5 unused days…0.96
    2Expenses §7 Unused travel budget…0.08

    The same labels also enforce permissions: a user only gets chunks they're allowed to see.

    Rerankers (cross-encoders) are slower than vector search, so a common pattern is: fetch 20 to 50 candidates cheaply, rerank them, send the best few.

    4. A stale index

    The leave policy was updated for 2026: carry-over is now 7 days. Nobody re-ran the pipeline.

    Problem: the file changed, the chunks didn't

    The fileLeave policy, 2026
    Up to 7 unused days
    ≠
    The vector databaseLeave §4, 2025
    Up to 5 unused days
    How many days can I carry over?
    Up to 5 days. [Leave policy §4]

    Correctly retrieved, correctly cited, and wrong.

    Fix: keep the index in sync

    Detect the changeCompare each file's fingerprint (hash) with the last run.
    Delete its old chunksEvery row from the leave policy goes.
    Run only that file againChunk, embed, store, as on page 05.
    Leave the rest aloneThe other 32 chunks are untouched.
    Also plan forDeleted files (delete their chunks), permission changes (update labels), and a new embedding model (re-embed everything, then switch).
    Stale chunks are one of the most common causes of confident, cited, wrong answers.

    5. No way to tell if it works

    An answer can read perfectly while the right chunk never reached the model.

    Problem: judging answers by eye

    My laptop was stolen. What do I do?
    Tell your line manager as soon as possible.

    Sounds reasonable. But IT handbook §8 says: report to IT security within one hour so the device can be locked. That chunk was never retrieved, and nobody noticed.

    Fix: test retrieval on its own

    Test questionChunk it should findIn top 3?
    Carry unused leave?Leave §4yes
    Part-time allowance?Leave §3yes
    Taxi to the airport?Expenses §5yes
    Stolen laptop?IT §8no
    Who signs HR-17?Leave §6yes
    80%found in the top 3. Fix the miss, then re-test after every change.
    This measure is called recall at k (here k = 3). Test retrieval before judging answers: an answer can't be better than the chunks it was given.

    6. Connected facts

    The answer needs two facts. Only one of them looks like the question.

    Question from someone in SalesWho approves my carry-over request?
    1
    Carry-over is approved by the employee's line manager.Leave policy §4: close to the question, retrieved
    2
    The Sales team's line manager is Sara Ahmed.Org chart: needed, but nothing like the question, so not retrieved
    The model's answerYour line manager approves it. (It can't say who.)
    Fact 2 only becomes relevant once fact 1 is known. The KAG paper puts it plainly: "simple vector-based retrieval is not suitable for multi-step reasoning". Searching, reading and searching again helps, at the cost of more model calls and time.

    7. Questions about the whole collection

    "What are the main complaints in three years of employee feedback?" The answer is spread across everything. Retrieval brings back a handful.

    20,000feedback chunks
    5retrieved for the answer
    0.025%of the collection seen
    The GraphRAG paper (Microsoft, 2024) calls these "global" questions: they are summarisation over the whole corpus, not retrieval, and plain RAG fails on them.

    8. Logic, numbers and rules

    "Which teams average more than 10 unused leave days?" Every row looks equally similar to the question. Similarity can't apply "more than 10".

    Team recordAverage unused days
    Sales12.4
    Finance6.1
    IT11.2
    HR4.0
    Operations9.8
    The model's answer, from the 3 retrieved rowsSales. (IT is missing: its row wasn't retrieved.)

    Counting, comparing, dates and rules need the data treated as data, not as text that sounds similar.

    The KAG paper notes that vector similarity is "insensitive to knowledge logic, such as numerical values, temporal relations, expert rules".

    What if the links were stored, not guessed?

    Text chunks hide the connections between facts. A knowledge graph stores them explicitly, so a question can follow them.

    Two ways to build on this idea come next: KAG, for precise answers that follow connections and rules, and GraphRAG, for questions about a whole collection.
